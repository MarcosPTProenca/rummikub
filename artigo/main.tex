\begin{abstract}
We study which simple strategy wins most often at two-player Rummikub and, more importantly, \emph{why}. In a from-scratch simulator of the official rules (106 tiles, two jokers, 14-tile hands, 30-point initial meld, table rearrangement), a paired round-robin of seven interpretable strategies (3{,}360 games) is won by the \emph{joker-saver}: it plays as many tiles as possible but spends a joker only if the move empties its hand (mean score $0.726\pm0.027$ against the field). Four ablations (13{,}000 games) isolate the cause: a soft joker penalty does not reproduce the effect, forbidding joker removal from the table does not remove it, it arises mostly after the opening meld, and forbidding the joker even as the final tile destroys it ($0.189\pm0.021$). The saver wins by holding a wildcard until it can go out ($71\%$ of its wins end with a joker, against $4\%$ for the control). That advantage is an artefact of counting games won. Official Rummikub is zero-sum in \emph{tile points}, with a joker left in hand counting $30$, and in $18{,}000$ games scored both ways the saver is first in games won and sixth of ten in points, $5.6$ [$3.8$, $7.4$] points per game below max-tiles, because it ends its losses holding $0.80$ jokers. Over sessions of ten hands with accumulated points, the format in which the game is actually played ($3{,}600$ sessions, $36{,}000$ hands), the inversion is complete: the saver wins the most hands ($5.4$ of $10$) and the fewest sessions ($0.288\pm0.030$), losing $72.5\%$ of sessions to max-tiles, and the session ranking is the exact reverse of the per-hand win ranking (Spearman $-1.00$). Releasing the joker before the end is first in both points and sessions. We then ask whether learning finds any of this. A tabular selector over the strategies, the Jev System One model (\texttt{jev-1.13.0}) as strategy selector or move chooser, a determinised Monte Carlo search and a small policy network trained by REINFORCE all fail to beat the best heuristics. Cloning the saver first and refining with group-relative advantages brings the network level with max-tiles and the saver (at best $0.550\pm0.040$ and $0.495\pm0.040$, exploratory); reward shaping, a wider league, a residual network, Monte Carlo returns, PPO (0.447 held out), a search that sees hidden hands, and rewarding the official score instead of the game outcome all add nothing. Training on larger tables helps from five players up and adds little at three and four. All claims are conditional on this simulator and strategy menu.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Rummikub is a tile game in which players empty their hands by forming runs and groups on a shared table. Its \emph{optimisation} subproblems are well studied: forming the best set of melds from a hand is polynomial-time solvable but becomes hard under generalisations \cite{vanrijn2016}, and integer linear programming solves full table-rearrangement problems \cite{denhertog2006}. Much less is known about which \emph{playing policies} win more often. Popular advice (``keep your jokers'') is widespread but, to our knowledge, rarely tested.
We ask three research questions.
\begin{description}\itemsep0pt
\item[RQ1] Among a menu of seven interpretable strategies, which wins most often --- and does the answer survive the scoring metric printed in the rules and the multi-hand format in which the game is played?
\item[RQ2] What \emph{mechanism} explains the winner's advantage?
\item[RQ3] Can reinforcement learning (RL), a language-model comparator or a small search agent, choosing a strategy or a move, do better than the best fixed one?
\end{description}
\paragraph{Status of the evidence.} RQ1 is exploratory, with two exceptions: the metric comparison of Section~\ref{sec:pontos} and the session experiment of Section~\ref{sec:sessao} have development and test seeds and criteria fixed before running. The mechanism experiments for RQ2 were designed \emph{after} seeing the RQ1 result, with four competing explanations and an informal prediction for each (Section~\ref{sec:mech}); they were not formally pre-registered, so we treat them as confirmatory only in the sense of a corrected multiple-comparison family. All results hold for our simulator and our menu of strategies; they are not claims about optimal Rummikub play.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
\paragraph{Rummikub.} The literature we found treats Rummikub as an optimisation problem: a polynomial-time algorithm for the one-hand puzzle \cite{vanrijn2016}, an integer-programming formulation for rearranging the table \cite{denhertog2006}, a thesis counting the hands that can be melded in one turn \cite{feijen2023}, and a recent bachelor thesis that reduces the memory of the puzzle solver and classifies puzzle difficulty \cite{wekken2025}. The last explicitly notes that the multi-player game (initial meld, table manipulation, strategy) has received little study and leaves strategy for future work. We found no study that evaluates learning or search agents on the full multi-player game against heuristic baselines; this is the result of a limited search (arXiv, AAAI/ICML proceedings and university thesis repositories; one earlier thesis was not accessible), not a proof of absence. The closest learning study we know is on a sibling game: Kotnik and Kalita compare temporal-difference learning with self-play against co-evolution of the value function in gin rummy, and co-evolution was superior \cite{kotnik2003}.
\paragraph{Learning in large-action card games.} DouZero reaches strong DouDizhu play by Monte Carlo returns, a network that scores encoded (state, action) pairs, and many parallel actors, which suits action sets that change from turn to turn \cite{zha2021}; our move network likewise scores concrete moves rather than a fixed action head. Suphx adds global reward prediction and oracle guiding (training with hidden information visible) to deep RL for mahjong \cite{li2020}. Policy-gradient self-play with a pool of past checkpoints as opponents is common practice in tabletop-game RL \cite{balla2024}, which our league runs (Section~\ref{sec:liga}) follow in spirit. A recent large-scale study finds generic PPO \cite{schulman2017} competitive with game-theoretic deep-RL methods in imperfect-information games \cite{rudolph2025}; we run a small PPO and a DouZero-style Monte Carlo variant (Section~\ref{sec:dmcppo}). Group-relative advantages, which replace a learned critic by standardising returns within a group of rollouts that share a prompt, were introduced for language-model training \cite{shao2024}; we use the same estimator with a group of games that share a deal. Our shaping term is a potential difference and therefore policy-invariant in the sense of \cite{ng1999}. ReBeL combines RL and search with convergence guarantees in two-player zero-sum games \cite{brown2020}. The closest positive result to our own setup is IRumAI, which warm-starts PPO by behaviour cloning in Indian Rummy and reports a $53.9\%$ win rate against the strongest search baseline unseen in training \cite{mohan2026}; our recipe is the same in outline and does not reproduce that outcome (Section~\ref{sec:disc}).
\paragraph{Search under hidden information.} Perfect-information Monte Carlo sampling, which our PIMC agent follows, is a practical approach but suffers \emph{strategy fusion}, since the search may choose different continuations in worlds the player cannot distinguish \cite{frank1998}; its empirical success has been analysed \cite{long2010}, and information-set MCTS searches over the player's information set instead of sampled worlds \cite{cowling2012}. We use neither a learned value function nor a model of the opponent's hand in the search; Section~\ref{sec:lim} lists them as untested.
\paragraph{Evaluation practice.} Our methodological conclusion is adjacent to work arguing that deep-RL comparisons rely too heavily on point estimates from few runs \cite{agarwal2021}. That critique concerns \emph{statistical} reliability at a fixed metric; we report a case where the metric itself, not its variance, reverses the conclusion (Sections~\ref{sec:pontos} and~\ref{sec:sessao}).

\section{Simulator and Strategies}
\paragraph{Game.} Two players in the main experiments (Section~\ref{sec:mesa} adds three and four); 106 tiles (4 colours $\times$ values 1--13 $\times$ 2 copies, plus 2 jokers); 14 tiles per hand. A turn either draws one tile or lays tiles down. The first lay-down (the \emph{opening meld}) must be worth at least 30 points using only the player's own tiles and may not touch the table. After the opening a player may lay several melds, extend melds, and rearrange the whole table, provided every meld on the table is valid at the end of the turn and at least one tile from the hand was played. The two jokers are shuffled with the other tiles, so a player can hold them from the deal or draw them later. A game ends when a hand is empty; if the pool is empty and both players pass in succession, the game ends as well. Our main experiments score this case as a draw ($0.5$); Section~\ref{sec:tie} repeats the tournament under the official tie-break (fewest points left in hand, a joker counting $30$) and under fewest tiles. Games are capped at 400 decisions (never reached). Seat~0 moves first.

\paragraph{Action space.} At each decision the simulator lists \emph{draw}, \emph{lay down new melds}, and \emph{rearrange} actions. Because exhaustive table search is expensive, rearrangements are a sample of at most 128 plans. This is the main fidelity limitation (Sections~\ref{sec:cap} and~\ref{sec:lim}).

\paragraph{Strategies.} Each chooses one of the legal actions.

random & uniform over legal actions, drawing included\\
max-tiles & play the move using the most hand tiles (ties: larger tile sum); draw if none\\
max-points & play the move with the largest tile sum\\
min-play & play the smallest legal move\\
joker-saver & like max-tiles, but consider only moves that use no joker, \emph{unless} some move empties the hand; otherwise draw\\

\end{table}

\paragraph{Q-meta.} A tabular Q-learner \cite{watkins1992} whose actions are the seven strategies plus ``draw'' (it \emph{selects} a strategy at each decision). State: opening status of both players, binned hand sizes, binned maximum playable tiles, pool size, whether a winning move exists, and whether the hand holds a joker. Reward is $+1/0/-1$ at the end only, $\gamma=1$, $\epsilon$ decays linearly from $0.3$ to $0.05$. It trains against opponents drawn uniformly from the seven strategies and is evaluated greedily.

\paragraph{Jev-meta.} At each decision \texttt{jev-1.13.0} (TypeSafe System One; fixed version) receives a JSON description of the state and a multiple-choice question over the same menu, with each option's concrete consequence in the current position. If its confidence is below a pre-fixed $0.3$, or the budget is exhausted, the agent falls back to max-tiles. Jev is a \emph{comparator}: it is not trained by RL.

\paragraph{Jev-moves.} A second Jev agent chooses the \emph{concrete move}. The state lists the rules, the hand (sorted), the table sets, the hand's point total, the opponent's hand size and opening status, and the pool size; every legal action of the turn, including ``draw'', is one option of a multiple-choice question (median $30$ options and maximum $177$ over $1{,}114$ sampled decisions, below the $255$ allowed). Each option states the tiles played from the hand, how many tiles remain, the table sets removed and added, and whether it empties the hand; sums and counts are computed in code. Option order is shuffled with a fixed seed. The answer indexes an already validated action, so no illegal move can be played. There is no confidence threshold; only transport failures, invalid answers or an exhausted budget fall back to max-tiles. Single-option turns are not sent. One prompt was used and was not tuned on results.

\paragraph{PIMC.} A search agent samples $D$ worlds consistent with what it can see (the opponent's hand and the pool are reshuffled; only the opponent's hand \emph{size} is used), scores at most $K=5$ candidate moves (the joker-saver's move, the best by tiles, and ``draw'') by finishing the game with the joker-saver on both sides under common random numbers, and plays the best; ties go to the joker-saver's move. Rollouts do not rearrange the table, which is $7\times$ faster; the real move list keeps rearrangements.

\section{Experimental Protocol and Statistics}\label{sec:protocol}
\paragraph{Pairing.} For every seed, both seatings are played with the same deal; the unit of analysis is the per-seed mean score of the strategy of interest (win $=1$, draw $=0.5$, loss $=0$). Pairing cancels the first-mover and deal effects: a strategy played against itself scores exactly $0.5$ by construction (control row in Table~\ref{tab:mec}). Seat~0 wins $50.8\%\pm1.5\%$ of tournament games.
\paragraph{Intervals and tests.} We report mean $\pm$ $1.96\times$ the standard error across seeds. $p$-values are two-sided normal-approximation tests of mean $=0.5$ over seeds ($n\ge 80$ seeds in all comparisons). In the mechanism family (13 groups, of which two are mirror controls with $p=1$ by construction) $p$-values are corrected with the Holm step-down procedure \cite{holm1979}; tournament intervals are descriptive and not corrected. For the pooled-field joker analysis (Section~\ref{sec:ini}) the two hands of a game and the games of a seed are correlated, so the intervals there are percentile bootstrap intervals that resample seeds ($1{,}000$ replicates), not the normal approximation.
\paragraph{Seeds and counts.} Tournament seeds $1000$--$1079$, mechanism seeds $70000$--$70499$, initial-joker seeds $50000$ and up, Jev-meta seeds $2000$--$2099$, Jev-moves seeds $4000$--$4069$ (plus $4000$--$4003$ in an 8-game pilot), PIMC and move-network evaluation seeds $100000$ and up (network training seeds $\ge 5{\times}10^6$), RL evaluation seeds $\ge 9{\times}10^6$ (training seeds are disjoint). Pooled-field joker analysis: seeds $90000$--$91999$ (4{,}000 games, 8{,}000 hands). Games: tournament 3{,}360 (21 pairs $\times$ 80 seeds $\times$ 2 seatings; replayed under two more tie-break rules); mechanism 13 groups $\times$ 500 seeds $\times$ 2 seatings (13{,}000); initial jokers 3 strategies $\times$ 2{,}000 seeds (6{,}000 mirror games); Q-meta 5 replicas $\times$ 1{,}200 training games and $5\times 7\times 20\times 2$ evaluation games; Jev 600; Jev-moves 280 (70 seeds $\times$ 2 opponents $\times$ 2 seatings); move network 7{,}000 evaluation games (7 opponents $\times$ 500 seeds $\times$ 2 seatings) after $36{,}000$ training games; PIMC 360 (60 seeds $\times$ 2 opponents $\times$ 2 seatings for $D=4$, and 30 seeds for $D=2$ and $D=8$ against the joker-saver); Q-meta under the official rule 5 replicas $\times$ 1{,}200 training games, evaluated with the same protocol. Later experiments: rearrangement-cap profiles $150{,}000$ and up, three- and four-player tables $120{,}000$ and up ($130{,}000$ and up for retrained agents), the $12$-strategy league $140{,}000$ and up, cloning demonstrations $6\times10^6$ and up, exploratory diagnosis of the cloned networks $160{,}000$ and up, confirmatory evaluation $170{,}000$ and up (group-advantage and rearrangement variants $175{,}000$ and up), the robustness matrix, shaping and wider-league runs $170{,}000$ and up, tile-counting runs $210{,}000$ and up, the official-points tournament on development seeds $220{,}000$--$220{,}119$ and test seeds $225{,}000$--$225{,}199$ ($45$ pairs $\times$ $200$ seeds $\times$ $2$ seats $=18{,}000$ games), the official-reward networks on development seeds $230{,}000$--$230{,}099$ ($4{,}800$ games; test seeds $235{,}000$+ unspent), and the sessions on development seeds $240{,}000$--$240{,}079$ and test seeds $245{,}000$--$245{,}119$ ($15$ pairs $\times$ $120$ sessions $\times$ $2$ arrangements $\times$ $10$ hands $=36{,}000$ hands; session-length sensitivity $248{,}000$+ and threshold diagnosis $249{,}000$+). Training seeds $0$--$2$ always start from the clone made with the same seed.

\subsection{RQ1: the heuristic tournament}

\subsection{RQ2: why does the joker-saver win?}\label{sec:mech}
The joker-saver differs from max-tiles in a single respect, how it treats jokers, so the question is which part of that behaviour matters. We state four candidate explanations and what each predicts. All comparisons are against max-tiles as opponent.
\begin{description}\itemsep0pt
\item[E1: a mild preference for spending fewer jokers.] Predicts that a soft penalty $\lambda$ per joker in the move score (maximising $n_{\text{tiles}}-\lambda n_{\text{jokers}}$) reproduces the gain.
\item[E2: recycling jokers on the table.] Rearranging lets a player take a joker out of a meld; saving jokers might make them less exposed to such steals. Predicts that forbidding the removal of a joker from its table meld removes the gain.
\item[E3: timing.] Predicts that sparing jokers only before the opening, or only after, isolates where the gain arises.
\item[E4: the joker as a finishing tile.] A joker completes almost any set, so holding one may make going out possible. Predicts that forbidding the joker even for the last move removes (and reverses) the gain, and that most wins of the saver end with a joker.
\end{description}

\paragraph{E1 is not supported.} Soft penalties of $0.5$--$4$ tiles per joker give scores of $0.500$--$0.508$, none distinguishable from $0.5$ after correction, and a joker \emph{bonus} is identical to the control. The reason is visible in Table~\ref{tab:mec}: with soft penalties the player still spends $0.77$ jokers per game, as often as the control ($0.78$); a preference only breaks ties between moves of equal size. The saver's behaviour is qualitatively different: it \emph{refuses} to play joker moves and draws instead, keeping $0.31$ jokers in hand at the end of an average game.
\paragraph{E2 is not supported.} When jokers can never be removed from table melds, the saver's score is $0.581\pm0.027$, not lower than in the standard rules ($0.568\pm0.027$). The saver does not owe its edge to joker recycling or table theft.
\paragraph{E3: the effect arises after the opening.} Saving jokers only before the opening gives nothing ($0.495\pm0.021$); saving only after it gives $0.537\pm0.022$ ($p_{\text{Holm}}=0.01$). The two timings do not add up to the full rule ($0.568$), so part of the effect depends on both phases acting together; we have not isolated it.
\paragraph{E4 is supported.} If the saver is forbidden to use a joker even to empty its hand, its score collapses to $0.189\pm0.021$ (Figure~\ref{fig:mec}): it ends games holding $0.86$ jokers on average and never wins by going out with one. Conversely, $71\%$ of the full saver's wins end with a joker in the final move (against $4\%$ for max-tiles). Saving jokers is therefore \emph{only} beneficial when paired with spending them to go out. Because the full-rule and never-play variants differ \emph{only} in that exception, this is a within-simulator causal test (an intervention on the policy), not just a correlation.

\paragraph{Explanation.} The joker is a wildcard; spent early it saves a tile's worth of play, but held it keeps the hand completable. A hand that has been reduced to a few awkward tiles can often be finished only with a wildcard, so the saver trades a little early progress for the ability to finish when the opportunity arises. This is our interpretation of E3--E4; it is consistent with the interventions, but we did not test the narrower claim about ``awkward last tiles''.

\subsection{RQ1b: the official scoring metric}\label{sec:pontos}
All results above score a game as win, draw or loss. Official Rummikub does not: the player who goes out scores the sum of the tiles left on every opponent's rack, each loser scores minus the value of its own rack, and a joker left in hand counts $30$, so a deal is zero-sum in \emph{tile points} \cite{pagat2024}. Winning more games and scoring more points are different objectives, and the joker-saver is exactly the policy for which they might disagree: it ends its losses holding $0.80$ jokers, which is $24$ points of liability per lost game. We therefore replayed the tournament computing both metrics \emph{from the same games}, so the two are perfectly paired, and added three strategies that decide when to let a joker go (defined before running, not tuned afterwards): \emph{saver-then-dump} (the joker-saver, but once the pool is empty it plays the largest move even with a joker), \emph{saver-dump-near} (the joker-saver, but it stops sparing jokers once an opponent is down to three tiles) and \emph{joker-spender} (prefers moves that spend jokers; a control for the opposite extreme, since the joker-bonus variants of Table~\ref{tab:mec} only break ties and are therefore identical to max-tiles). The three criteria below were fixed before running, on development seeds ($220{,}000$+, $120$ seeds); the test seeds ($225{,}000$+, $200$ seeds $\times$ $2$ seats, $18{,}000$ games) were spent once, with Holm over the three.

The ranking changes (Table~\ref{tab:pontos}, Figure~\ref{fig:pontos}). The joker-saver is first by win score ($0.680\pm0.017$) and sixth of ten by official points ($+13.3\pm1.5$), below max-tiles and max-points ($+19.5$ and $+19.6$) which it beats in games won. Spearman's rank correlation between the two orderings is $0.64$, against $0.96$ between the end-of-game rules of Section~\ref{sec:tie}: the choice of metric reorders the top far more than the choice of tie-break does. Against a common field of five strategies, paired by seed, max-tiles is $+5.59$ [$+3.78$, $+7.39$] points above the joker-saver while being $-0.013\pm0.018$ below it in win score (criterion: $\ge+3.0$ with a positive interval; met, $p_{\text{Holm}}<0.001$). Head-to-head the saver wins $0.573\pm0.044$ of the games and still loses $5.4\pm3.1$ points per game.

The cause is the mirror image of the mechanism of Section~\ref{sec:mech}. Decomposing the saver's points against the common field: it wins $77.8\%$ of those games, and when it wins it collects $+48.0$ points, the same as max-tiles ($+47.9$) — holding a joker buys the win, not a bigger win. When it loses it pays $-43.3$, against $-14.6$ for max-tiles, and $24$ of those $29$ extra points are the retained joker. The same rule that converts jokers into wins converts losses into expensive losses, and under the official metric the second effect is the larger one.

Intervening on the policy confirms the joker is the channel, with a caveat. \emph{Saver-then-dump} ends its losses with $0.64$ jokers instead of $0.80$ and gains $+2.83$ [$+1.99$, $+3.67$] points over the saver while \emph{also} gaining $+0.019$ [$+0.012$, $+0.025$] in win score: dumping the joker once the pool is empty is free. The gain in points did not meet the $+3.0$ we had fixed, so we report it as a near miss rather than a confirmed effect; the no-regression criterion in win score was met. \emph{Saver-dump-near} dumps much earlier, ends with $0.09$ jokers and pays $-17.7$ per loss instead of $-43.3$: it is first of all ten in points ($+19.8\pm1.1$) and only sixth in win score ($0.622\pm0.021$, $-0.019\pm0.014$ against the saver). So the two objectives are reachable, by different policies, and the gap between them is governed by when the joker is released. \emph{Joker-spender} is inert: under max-tiles play a joker is in hand in $2.6\%$ of decisions and a choice between a joker move and a non-joker move of the same size arises in $0.1\%$, so it played identically to max-tiles in all $1{,}440$ comparable games. This is a property of the action distribution, not a failure of the strategy.

We draw two conclusions. First, the answer to ``which strategy wins'' depends on what ``wins'' means, and the popular advice to save jokers is defensible only under the game-count metric: under the metric printed in the rules it costs about six points a game against simply playing the most tiles. Second, the finishing-joker mechanism of Section~\ref{sec:mech} is not overturned; it is priced. Holding a wildcard is a bet that pays a win and charges $30$ points when it fails. All the reinforcement-learning experiments below optimise the $\pm1$ game outcome, which we now know is the secondary metric; training on points is untested and is the first item of Section~\ref{sec:lim}.

\subsection{RQ1c: sessions of several hands}\label{sec:sessao}
Rummikub is normally played as a \emph{session}: several deals, points accumulated, highest total wins. Summing independent hands narrows the distribution around the mean, and the joker-saver's mean is negative, so the session should amplify what Section~\ref{sec:pontos} found. We built a session engine: $K$ hands, official points accumulated, the highest total wins, the starting seat alternating each hand as the turn to start passes in the rules (with $K$ even this cancels the first-mover effect inside the session), and the seed of hand $k$ derived from (session seed, $k$), so different strategies meet the \emph{same} hands in the same position of the session. A session also creates information that no single-hand strategy has, the accumulated balance, so we added \emph{session-adaptive}: when it is behind by more than one joker ($-30$ points, fixed in advance) it plays the joker-saver, maximising its chance of winning the hand; otherwise it plays saver-dump-near. Six strategies, $15$ pairs, $K=10$, both seat arrangements; development seeds $240{,}000$+ ($80$ sessions per pair) and test seeds $245{,}000$+ ($120$ sessions, $3{,}600$ sessions, $36{,}000$ hands), spent once, with Holm over the two primary criteria fixed before running.

The inversion is complete (Table~\ref{tab:sessao}, Figure~\ref{fig:sessao}). The joker-saver wins $5.39$ of the $10$ hands in a session, more than any other strategy, and wins $0.288\pm0.030$ of the sessions, fewer than any other strategy. Head-to-head against max-tiles it takes $5.53$ hands out of $10$ and loses $72.5\%$ of the sessions ($0.725$ [$0.668$, $0.782$] for max-tiles, criterion $\ge0.55$ with the interval above $0.5$; met, $p_{\text{Holm}}<0.001$) at $-75.6\pm14.9$ points per session. The ranking of sessions matches the per-hand \emph{points} ranking of Section~\ref{sec:pontos} (Spearman $+0.90$) and is the \emph{exact reverse} of the per-hand win ranking (Spearman $-1.00$) on the five strategies the two experiments share. The effect grows with the session, as the arithmetic predicts: max-tiles beats the saver in $0.558\pm0.089$ of one-hand sessions, $0.754\pm0.077$ at $K=5$ and $0.754\pm0.077$ at $K=20$, with the points margin rising from $+14$ to $+142$.

\emph{Session-adaptive} failed, and failed in the informative direction. It loses to saver-dump-near, the fixed strategy it switches away from ($0.444$ [$0.414$, $0.473$]; criterion not met, and the deficit is itself significant, $p_{\text{Holm}}=0.0002$). The rule is not inert: it is behind the $-30$ threshold in $26\%$ of hands, so it does change posture often. Switching to joker-saver play when behind buys hands ($4.96$ per session, the most of the four strong strategies) and costs points, which is the same trade the whole section is about; chasing a deficit by saving jokers digs the hole deeper. The accumulated balance is real information that this rule turns into a loss, and we report it as a negative result rather than tuning the threshold, which was fixed in advance and tried at one value.

Two readings follow. First, the practical answer to the original question changes with the format: in a single hand scored by games, save your jokers; in a session scored by points, which is how the game is actually played, release them before the end --- saver-dump-near is first in sessions won ($0.655\pm0.028$) and the joker-saver is last. Second, this is a cautionary measurement about benchmark design rather than about Rummikub: a $0.573$ per-hand win rate, the kind of number this literature routinely reports as an improvement, corresponds here to losing three sessions out of four. The quantity that is cheap to measure and the quantity the game rewards were anti-correlated on our menu.

\paragraph{Jev-moves.} Over 280 games Jev answered about $6{,}000$ decisions (about $2{,}000$ input tokens per call, US\$0.0018 per game, US\$0.51 in total); the fallback was used $7$ times. Its mean confidence was $0.38$, low as expected with about $30$ options. Its choice coincided with max-tiles' choice in $40\%$ of decisions, it drew in $3\%$ and played a joker in only $3$--$4\%$, so it neither reproduces the biggest-move rule nor the finishing-joker rule. It is clearly worse than max-tiles and worse than the joker-saver. The prompt gave the rules and the consequence of every option but no strategy advice; other prompts (for instance one that stresses emptying the hand) were not tried.
\paragraph{PIMC.} With $D=4$ worlds the search is statistically indistinguishable from max-tiles ($0.542$) and from the joker-saver ($0.433$, whose interval includes $0.5$), but with $60$ seeds the power is low. More worlds did not help ($D=8$: $0.400$) and fewer clearly hurt ($D=2$: $0.267$). The cost is about $105$\,s per game for $D=4$ against about $1.3$\,s for a heuristic. Because the rollout policy is itself the joker-saver, the search can at best correct its choices at the margin, and ties are resolved in its favour; we therefore read this as ``search with this rollout does not beat the joker-saver'', not as a bound on search in general.

\paragraph{Move network.} To remove the strategy menu from the learner we trained a small policy that scores each legal move directly: a network with $15$ features per move (move size, jokers used, hand and pool sizes, opening status, \dots), one hidden layer of $16$ tanh units and a softmax over the legal moves, written in plain Python. It was trained by REINFORCE with an exponential-moving-average baseline, Adam ($3{\times}10^{-3}$) and an entropy bonus ($0.01$) for $1{,}500$ iterations of $24$ games ($36{,}000$ games), with reward $\pm1$ at the end of the game. Opponents were drawn from a league: $50\%$ the seven heuristics, $25\%$ the current network, $25\%$ one of its last ten snapshots. It is evaluated greedily on $500$ held-out seeds per opponent, both seatings ($7{,}000$ games, none truncated). It plays like max-tiles: $0.493$ and $0.492$ against max-tiles and max-points (both intervals include $0.5$), where max-tiles itself scores $0.506$ against max-points. It beats the weaker heuristics by about as much as max-tiles does ($0.732$ against random, where max-tiles scores $0.731$; $0.634$ against min-play, $0.656$). Against the joker-saver it scores $0.403\pm0.030$, below $0.5$ and close to what max-tiles obtains ($0.425$ in the tournament, $80$ seeds). The finishing-joker rule was not learned: it went out with a joker in $2.9\%$ of games (max-tiles $3.5\%$, joker-saver $41\%$) and played $0.80$ jokers per game (max-tiles $0.83$). The training-time win rate against heuristics rose slowly from $0.59$ to $0.64$ (blocks of $250$ iterations); it is measured on a changing league and is not evidence of strength. This is one run of one architecture and one learning rate, with no hyper-parameter search, so we read it as ``this learner, with this budget, converges to max-tiles-like play'' and not as a limit of reinforcement learning.

\paragraph{Starting from imitation, and eighteen variants (exploratory).} Because the network trained from scratch never found the finishing-joker rule, we first asked whether the features can represent it at all. We cloned the joker-saver by supervised learning on $10{,}587$ of its decisions ($300$ games against the seven heuristics, with rearrangement; the same likelihood as REINFORCE with advantage $1$). The clone agrees with the saver's choice in $82\%$ of held-out decisions and goes out with a joker in $70\%$ of its wins against the saver ($71\%$ for the saver itself). So the $15$ features and one hidden layer suffice to represent the mechanism; what the from-scratch run lacked was the search for it. Cloning agreement stays at $0.82$ for every width ($16$, $64$, $256$ units) and every feature set ($15$, $19$, $23$), so capacity is not what caps imitation either.

From those clones we ran the eighteen variants of Table~\ref{tab:rl}, each with the protocol and the success criterion fixed in writing before running: three training seeds unless noted, $300$ iterations of $24$ games, learning rate $3\times10^{-3}$, greedy evaluation on fresh seeds, paired by deal and seat. \emph{First, one positive estimate did not replicate, and we report the failure.} On the first training seed the cloned-then-refined network scored $0.605\pm0.07$ against max-tiles and $0.545\pm0.07$ against the saver, the only result in this project that appeared to beat both. Two further training seeds on fresh seeds gave $0.509\pm0.045$ and $0.487\pm0.045$: the first number was a favourable draw of seeds and run, selected by looking. PPO repeated the pattern at larger scale, reaching $0.500$ against the saver on development seeds and $0.447$ on held-out seeds spent once. Both are the expected behaviour when the best of several candidates is chosen on the data used to choose it.

\emph{Second, group-relative advantages \cite{shao2024} are the only change that did anything, and it is small.} With four games sharing each deal and the advantage standardised within the group, the agent scores $0.550\pm0.040$ against max-tiles ($p=0.014$ uncorrected), the only interval in this paper excluding $0.5$ against max-tiles. We read it cautiously: it is one of four comparisons, and its difference from the same clones trained with a moving-average baseline on the same seeds ($0.520\pm0.040$) is not itself significant. The more consistent effect is on behaviour: with grouping the agent goes out with a joker in $59$--$66\%$ of its wins in all three trainings, against $14$--$43\%$ without it, so the lower-variance signal preserves what the clone knew rather than teaching something new.

\emph{Third, the obvious explanations for the ceiling are each ruled out.} Hidden information is not binding: a determinised search given the \emph{true} opponent hand and draw order, rather than sampled ones, scores $0.508\pm0.089$ against the saver. Capacity is not binding: $64$ units is clearly worse ($-0.088$) and $256$ is no better ($+0.012$), and the $64$-unit loss is not unstable training (its training win rate rises like the others) but overfitting to the training league, visible only against an external evaluator. The state is not binding either: nineteen opponent features and twenty-three with tile counting leave the result unchanged under the curriculum regime. Opponent diversity is not binding: a nine-strategy training league moves nothing ($0.546$ against max-tiles, against $0.517$ before widening). And a residual network that starts \emph{as} the saver, adding its score to a bonus on the saver's move, never departs from it --- in none of $270$ greedy decisions per seed at $\beta\in\{1.5,4\}$, and in $7$ of $225$ with $\beta=1$ and $6.7$ times the games --- so the policy-gradient signal at this budget cannot even find a local improvement on the heuristic it starts from.

\paragraph{Robustness to opponents never seen in training (exploratory).} The group-advantage networks and, for contrast, the moving-average ones were played against all twelve strategies of Section~\ref{sec:liga} ($40$ paired seeds, both seats, three training seeds pooled; Table~\ref{tab:robustez}). The ``drop'' between seen and unseen opponents cannot be read directly, because the unseen set is harder for everyone --- the joker-saver itself falls from $0.740$ to $0.535$ --- so we compare each agent with the heuristic references on the same opponents. On unseen opponents the group-advantage agent scores $0.532$, the same as max-tiles ($0.532$) and the joker-saver ($0.535$); on seen opponents its mean ($0.693$) is $0.03$--$0.05$ below theirs. Nothing breaks and nothing improves: the network behaves like max-tiles against everyone.

\paragraph{Deep Monte Carlo and PPO (confirmatory for PPO).}\label{sec:dmcppo} Two variants transfer techniques from other games to our network, both from the clone, with a league of heuristics, the current network and past checkpoints as opponents, $300$ iterations of $24$ games, learning rate $10^{-3}$, four gradient steps per iteration and three training seeds each, fixed before running. Deep Monte Carlo \cite{zha2021} regresses the score of each played move to the final return; its value loss ends near $1.0$, the variance of the returns, so the regression never learned to predict them, and it stays below the saver ($0.472\pm0.068$). PPO \cite{schulman2017} uses a separate value network on a seven-feature state, a normalised advantage, ratio clipping at $0.2$ and a KL penalty ($0.1$) towards the clone. On development seeds it was the only candidate on par with both heuristics (lower bound $\ge0.45$), so we spent the held-out seeds on it once ($150$ seeds, both seats, three training seeds, $900$ games per opponent; intervals over the $300$ deals, which the training seeds share). \paragraph{Stress training on tables of up to ten players.}\label{sec:estresse} The network trained for two players loses ground at tables of three and four, so we extended the engine to two to ten players (one set of $106$ tiles per four players, $14$ tiles each; ten players use $318$ tiles and six jokers) and trained with a curriculum: each training game draws its table size uniformly from $2,\dots,10$. Everything else repeats the group-relative recipe ($K=4$, $300$ iterations of $24$ games, learning rate $3\times10^{-3}$, start from the clone, three training seeds), so the control is the same network trained at two players only. The protocol was fixed before training, and the plan's five variants were already used, so this counts as a separate hypothesis. Table~\ref{tab:estresse} shows the development seeds ($176{,}000$+; intervals over deals, paired with the control; tables of six and ten use only $20$ seeds). The curriculum helps at larger tables, by $+0.040$ at three players (interval includes zero), $+0.066$ at four, $+0.050$ at six and $+0.090$ at ten, in the mixed field, and ends above the joker-saver at six and ten players. It costs at two players: $0.430$ against the saver [$0.367$, $0.493$], not on par by the margin set in advance, and $0.493$ against max-tiles. Our pre-set criterion required a gain of at least $0.03$ with an interval above zero at both three and four players, and no loss at two; it was not met (three players fails, two players fails), so the held-out seeds were left untouched. The pattern, however, suggested a new hypothesis, which we stated before touching them: the curriculum beats the control in the mixed field at four, six and ten players (Holm over the three tests; $40$ to $60$ seeds, both seats at every seat, networks unchanged), with the loss at two players as a descriptive check. At two players the curriculum is again lower, $0.427$ against the saver [$0.364$, $0.489$], $-0.051$ against the control (interval includes zero). Against the saver itself the curriculum is ahead at six and ten players ($+0.074$, $+0.063$) and level at three and four. The effects are small ($0.04$ to $0.06$ points) and, at four players, smaller than on the development seeds ($0.066$). The treated network saw large tables in training and the control never did, so the gain is in-distribution rather than a sign of transfer; training up to six players and testing at ten would be the test for that, and we did not run it.

\paragraph{Training only on harder tables.} The curriculum included the tables we measured, so we asked the question the other way round: train only at six to ten players (never two to five) and test at three and four. The recipe is the same (three training seeds), the criterion was set before training, and the development result did not meet it (gain $+0.039$ at three players with the interval including zero, $+0.061$ at four). We then tested, once and on held-out seeds ($195{,}000$+), the post hoc hypothesis that this network beats the two-player control at three and four players; it did not. The difference in the mixed field is $+0.035$ [$-0.020$, $0.090$] at three and $-0.002$ [$-0.046$, $0.041$] at four (Holm-adjusted $p=0.42$ and $0.91$). At five players, never seen in training and not part of the criterion, the gain is $+0.095$ [$0.047$, $0.143$] (development: $+0.060$). The network trained only on hard tables is indistinguishable from the curriculum network at every table size (differences of at most $0.01$ with narrow intervals), so what matters is having seen large tables, not the ones measured. The same seed set also weakens the earlier curriculum result: at four players the curriculum scores $-0.004$ against the control (development $+0.066$, first test $+0.049$), and the control's cost at two players disappears ($0.472$ for the curriculum and $0.475$ for the hard-table network against the saver, against $0.452$ for the control). Pooling the three seed sets (descriptively, since the development set suggested the hypothesis) the curriculum gains $+0.043$ [$0.014$, $0.071$] at three players and $+0.037$ [$0.012$, $0.062$] at four. We read this as a small gain at three and four players, about the size of the seed-to-seed variation, and a clear one from five players up.

\paragraph{Training on the official reward (exploratory, negative).} Every learned agent above maximises the $\pm1$ game outcome, which Section~\ref{sec:pontos} shows is the secondary metric. If the eleven null results are a limit of the objective rather than of the method, replacing the reward by the official score should fix them: the difference between policies that are good at points and good at games is \emph{when to release the joker}, a decision the network already sees (its move vector contains the jokers used and whether the move empties the hand) but never had a reason to use. We therefore retrained with reward $\text{points}/60$ (the liability of two jokers, which puts the reward on the $\pm1$ scale the learning rate expects), from the same three clones, with the same recipe that is the paper's best ($K{=}4$ group advantages, $300$ iterations of $24$ games, learning rate $3\times10^{-3}$, $N=2$). The control is the three group-advantage networks of Table~\ref{tab:rl}, unchanged. Criteria were fixed before running: a gain of $\ge3$ points over the control against max-tiles, a non-negative score in points against max-tiles, and no regression below $0.45$ in win score; development seeds $230{,}000$+ ($100$ seeds, both seats, four opponents, $4{,}800$ games), with test seeds $235{,}000$+ reserved.

The reward changes behaviour in the predicted direction and does not change the outcome. The trained networks end a game holding $0.22$ jokers against $0.28$ for the control, go out with a joker in $58\%$ of their wins against $69\%$, and pay $-33.2$ points per loss against $-38.7$: they learned to release the wildcard earlier, which is exactly what Section~\ref{sec:pontos} shows is worth points. But the size is small. Against max-tiles they gain $+1.7$ [$-0.7$, $+4.1$] points over the control, short of the $+3$ we had fixed and with an interval containing zero, and they score $-5.1$ [$-8.3$, $-2.0$] points against max-tiles in absolute terms, so they remain clearly \emph{below} it on the very metric they optimise. Win score is unharmed ($0.530$ [$0.479$, $0.581$], the only criterion met). Pooled over the four opponents the gain is $+3.3\pm1.6$ points with no change in win score ($+0.002\pm0.020$), and it is consistent across the three training seeds ($-4.8$, $-5.0$, $-5.6$ against max-tiles, against $-7.8$, $-5.3$, $-7.4$ for the control), so the effect is real but an order of magnitude smaller than the $5.6$-point gap it would have to close. Training-time reward rose from $+0.08$ to $+0.18$, so the runs did learn. We did not spend the held-out seeds.

This closes the objection that the negative RL results are an artefact of the reward: with this architecture, this budget and this recipe, the network fails to beat the best heuristic under \emph{both} scoring metrics, and the one behaviour it does acquire from the official reward (dumping the joker earlier) is a behaviour we can also write in one line as a heuristic, where it is worth about $4.5$ points (Section~\ref{sec:pontos}). What limits these agents is not which number we reward. Section~\ref{sec:orcamento} removes the budget from the same list.

\paragraph{Counting hidden tiles (exploratory, negative).} None of our strategies or features knew how many copies of each tile can still appear, although the table and one's own hand fix that count exactly (two copies per colour and number per 106-tile set, minus what is visible). We tried it in two ways, with the protocol fixed before running ($120$ seeds and both seats per cell, seeds $210{,}000$+, N$=2$ to $4$, pairing as above). \emph{Offensive:} \texttt{conta\_rara} breaks ties of max-tiles by the pairs in the remaining hand that can still be completed, each weighted by the copies still hidden. \emph{Defensive:} \texttt{defensiva\_copia} holds back tiles whose last copies are in one's hand and that would extend a table combination, accepting fewer tiles played unless the move empties the hand; \texttt{defensiva\_numero}, which holds back numbers whose group the opponents cannot complete, fires in $0.3\%$ of decisions and was kept descriptive. None met the bar. At two players \texttt{conta\_rara} scores $0.408$ [$0.346$, $0.471$] against the saver and $0.512$ against the same-length-move strategy without counting; \texttt{defensiva\_copia} differs from the saver by $+0.002$ [$-0.061$, $0.064$] at three and $+0.007$ [$-0.045$, $0.060$] at four players. We then added four counting features to the move vector (mean rarity of the tiles spent and of those kept, share of blocking tiles spent, number of blocking types held; $19\to23$) and retrained with the same recipe, three seeds and two regimes: twenty-three minus nineteen features, same regime and same hands, is $-0.007$ [$-0.044$, $0.030$] at two players (two-player regime) and $+0.009$ [$-0.012$, $0.030$] at four (curriculum). Cloning agreement stays at $0.82$. We did not spend the held-out seeds. Figure~\ref{fig:negativas} places this result beside the two other feature-set hypotheses: all three sit on zero. Counting is a real information source that the heuristics and the network did not turn into wins; with the games here, the joker rule still carries the advantage.

\subsection{Scaling the training budget: the one variable we never moved}\label{sec:orcamento}
Every variant of Table~\ref{tab:rl} trained on $7{,}200$ games ($300$ iterations of $24$), and Section~\ref{sec:lim} declared that budget a limitation without ever measuring it. DouZero uses on the order of $10^9$ games \cite{zha2021}; IRumAI uses $327.7$ million PPO steps on a single GH200 \cite{mohan2026}. We therefore repeated the paper's best recipe (clone of the joker-saver, group-relative advantages with $K{=}4$, official points as reward) changing \emph{only} the budget: $4{,}000$ iterations of $48$ games, $192{,}000$ games per training seed, three seeds, $576{,}000$ games, $9.9$~h of wall-clock on six CPU cores. Criteria were fixed before running: a gain of $\ge4$ points over the same recipe at the old budget against the joker-saver, a non-negative score in points, and no regression against max-tiles. Development seeds $250{,}000$+, test seeds $255{,}000$+ reserved.

Against the joker-saver the scaled agent scores $+4.69$ [$+2.10$, $+7.28$] official points ($150$ hands, $5{,}400$ games), the first learned agent in this project whose interval excludes zero against the champion on the metric the rules use. The gain over the same recipe at $7{,}200$ games is $+2.83$ [$-0.84$, $+6.51$], short of the $+5$ we had required, so the budget helps but its isolated effect does not reach the size we demanded. The third criterion failed because of how we wrote it: we required non-negative points against max-tiles, and the agent scores $-2.79$ [$-4.27$, $-1.30$]; but both controls are also negative there ($-4.75$ and $-8.86$), because max-tiles is the strongest agent in points (Section~\ref{sec:pontos}). We had asked for a condition no learned agent in this paper ever satisfied, and we report it that way rather than rewrite the criterion after seeing the number. The held-out seeds were not spent.

\emph{The mechanism is the interesting part, and it inverts the usual story.} The scaled agent \emph{wins fewer hands} than its own control ($0.390$ against $0.487$) and still scores more points, because it loses far more cheaply: $-18.1$ points per loss against $-42.5$. In defeat it ends with $2.78$ tiles and $0.011$ jokers in hand, against $4.31$ tiles and $0.604$ jokers for the control. It learned to release the joker and empty its hand \emph{before} losing instead of chasing the win --- a policy none of our heuristics implements, and the cleanest confirmation of this paper's central claim: the metric chosen determines the optimal policy.

\emph{The budget stopped binding early.} The training return saturated near $+10$ points around iteration $450$ of $4{,}000$ and oscillated without trend afterwards; evaluating eleven checkpoints against the joker-saver gives $+3.2$ (iteration $250$), $+10.3$ ($1{,}000$), $+2.3$ ($2{,}250$), $+6.1$ ($4{,}000$). The last $167{,}000$ games bought nothing. Budget is therefore ruled out as the explanation for the eighteen null results, alongside capacity, state, hidden information, reward and opponent diversity.

\paragraph{Is the learned agent the best player? No --- it ties with the best.} A duel against two opponents cannot support that claim, so we entered the three networks into the common-field tournament of Section~\ref{sec:pontos}: thirteen competitors, every pair, the same hands, both seats, $9{,}360$ games, seeds $270{,}000$+ (Table~\ref{tab:campo_rl}, Figure~\ref{fig:campo_rl}). The network ranks \emph{first} in official points, $+16.96\pm1.38$ against max-tiles' $+15.60\pm1.44$. That ranking is a trap. The paired comparison on identical hands against the same nine common opponents gives \textbf{$-1.69$ [$-3.70$, $+0.31$]} --- the interval includes zero and the sign is negative. In the head-to-head duel the network scores $-0.60$ [$-2.75$, $+1.55$] points and wins $0.492$ of hands: a technical tie on both metrics. The rank-order lead is an artefact of each agent facing a slightly different field.

What does survive is narrower and still worth stating: the network beats the joker-saver in points head-to-head, $+5.59$ [$+1.42$, $+9.75$], while winning only $0.411$ of the hands. It is the only learned agent here to manage that. But the best Rummikub player in this study remains a three-line heuristic, and in win rate the network is sixth of eleven. Reinforcement learning with $27\times$ the budget reached parity with max-tiles, not superiority over it.

\subsection{Structured board representation: a negative about our compute, not about the game}\label{sec:conv}
With budget ruled out, the remaining untested input is the \emph{shape} of the representation. Every feature vector in this paper is an aggregate scalar ($n/14$, $\text{sum}/60$, $\min(\text{hands})/14$), which destroys the two relations that define Rummikub: adjacent numbers form runs, and distinct colours of one number form groups. IRumAI's ablation is the most direct external evidence that this matters --- replacing their dual-branch convolutional encoder with a width-matched MLP costs $8$ to $11$ percentage points, more than removing reward shaping ($4.9$) or the behaviour-cloning warm start, and the largest single contribution they report \cite{mohan2026}. The geometry transfers exactly: their $4$ suits $\times$ $13$ ranks is our $4$ colours $\times$ $13$ numbers, so a $1\times3$ convolution along numbers sees runs and a $4\times1$ convolution along colours sees groups.

We implemented it: a $7\times4\times13$ tensor per move (remaining hand, tiles played, final table, unseen tiles, tiles that would complete a meld, tiles already paired, table covered by a valid meld), two convolutional branches with $16$ filters each, max-pooling over colours, a $64$-unit dense trunk, the original fifteen features kept as scalars so the comparison adds structure rather than trading information. The analytic gradient was verified against finite differences ($12$ components, $|\Delta|<2\times10^{-3}$).

The plan's third criterion was a gate: cloning on the new architecture had to reach the $0.82$ agreement with the joker-saver that \emph{every} previous architecture reached ($16$, $64$ and $256$ units; $15$, $19$ and $23$ features --- all $0.82$). It reached $0.706$ on held-out decisions with the paper's $10{,}609$ cloning samples, and $0.745$ with three times the data. The diagnosis is variance, not bias: the convolutional network has $28{,}849$ parameters against the MLP's $273$ ($106\times$), i.e.\ $2.7$ parameters per cloning sample against $0.026$, and tripling the data raised held-out agreement while closing the train--test gap from $0.097$ to $0.043$.

Extrapolating that curve puts the $0.82$ gate somewhere between $10^5$ and $10^6$ cloning samples. Nine hundred cloning games already took $64$ minutes; the comparison is with IRumAI's $10{,}000$ demonstration games \emph{plus} $327.7$ million PPO steps at $2{,}400$ steps/s on a GH200, against our six games per second on six CPU cores with \texttt{listar\_acoes\_validas} consuming $98\%$ of the time. That is three to four orders of magnitude, and patience does not close it. We stopped at the gate and spent neither the development nor the held-out seeds.

This negative is categorically different from the others in this paper. Those are claims about Rummikub; this is a claim about our compute budget. \textbf{It does not license the statement that convolutional representations fail to help in Rummikub} --- the architecture never left imitation, so the plan's question remains open. We report it because an unanswered question that consumed a working implementation is itself information for whoever tries next, and because the implementation is available and verified.

\paragraph{On joint-embedding predictive architectures.} JEPA \cite{assran2023} is the obvious next idea and, we argue, the wrong one for this problem as posed. A JEPA predicts the \emph{representation} of a masked or future observation instead of its raw content, with an EMA target encoder and asymmetric branches to avoid collapse; the gain is discarding unpredictable surface detail. That gain is defined against high-dimensional observations: I-JEPA and V-JEPA work on images and video, and the one study we found applying JEPA to reinforcement learning uses pixel CartPole, where the observation has $720{,}000$ dimensions and the true state has four \cite{zhang2025jeparl}. Our move tensor has $364$ entries and is already the structured state; there is no surface detail to discard. The same study reports that JEPA without reinforcement gradients collapses to a constant embedding unless a variance regulariser is added, which is a sizeable extra failure mode for an architecture we cannot yet train to the imitation baseline. There is a genuinely interesting variant for our setting --- latents that carry a \emph{belief} over hidden continuations, since the opponent's hand is exactly such a hidden mode \cite{uwmjepa2026} --- but the published demonstrations are oscillator and CartPole-scale, and our bottleneck would be identical to Section~\ref{sec:conv}: parameters per sample, not ideas.

\paragraph{The real next step is the simulator, not the agent.} \texttt{listar\_acoes\_validas} consumes $98\%$ of every game, enumerating table rearrangements in pure Python; a game with rearrangement costs $1{,}006$~ms against $167$~ms without. Memoisation by table signature, dominance pruning, or the enumeration in C would multiply the effective budget of every future experiment, and is a precondition for any architecture with more than a few thousand parameters. We report this as the recommendation rather than as a result.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:disc}
The simulation supports a narrow, mechanistic answer to ``what wins'': \emph{play the biggest legal move, but keep jokers until one of them lets you go out} --- if winning means winning a single hand by game count. The three weaker explanations (soft preference, table recycling, timing alone) each fail, while the finishing-tile intervention both removes and reverses the effect. Practically, the common advice ``save your jokers'' is only half right twice over. First, unconditional saving is far worse than not saving ($0.189$): the condition ``unless it wins the game'' is the whole content of the rule. Second, the rule itself is an artefact of the measurement. Under the tile-point scoring printed in the rules, keeping a joker costs about six points a game against simply playing the most tiles, because the $30$-point liability in the lost games outweighs the extra games won (Section~\ref{sec:pontos}); over sessions of ten hands, the format in which the game is actually played, the same policy wins the most hands and the fewest sessions (Section~\ref{sec:sessao}). The mechanism is the same object seen from two sides: a held wildcard is a bet that pays a win and charges $30$ points when it fails, and which side dominates is decided by the scoring rule and the format, not by the game. Releasing the joker before the end improves points and sessions at once, so the frontier is not a strict trade-off everywhere. The same picture appears from the side of the tile: a joker, whether dealt or drawn, adds a clearly positive amount only for the joker-saver (Section~\ref{sec:ini}); for a player that spends jokers freely it is worth about nothing. None of this hinges on how a stalled pool is scored (Section~\ref{sec:tie}).

The measurement lesson generalises beyond this game. A per-hand win rate of $0.573$, the kind of margin this literature routinely reports as an improvement, corresponded here to losing three sessions out of four, and the session ranking was the exact reverse of the per-hand win ranking on the five strategies measured both ways. Game count is the cheap, low-variance, conventional metric; points over a session is the one the rules define. On our menu they were anti-correlated, and no amount of extra seeds on the cheap metric would have revealed it.

The negative RL, Jev and search results are also informative. A tabular selector over a small state and a general-purpose language model choosing a strategy both gravitated to max-tiles-like behaviour; neither found the finishing-joker rule, which a human analyst reading the heuristic tournament did. Giving Jev the whole legal move list did not help: it was worse than the fixed heuristics, and a determinised search that rolls out with the joker-saver only matched it. A small policy network trained end to end on the move list, with no strategy menu, ended up at the same place: statistically level with max-tiles, below the joker-saver, and almost never going out with a joker. Against the joker-saver, every learned or searching agent except PIMC with $D=2$ scores between $0.40$ and $0.43$; the exception among learned agents is the one started from a clone of the joker-saver ($0.487\pm0.045$, Section~\ref{sec:moves}), which ties with it, and also with group-relative advantages ($0.495\pm0.040$). The finishing-joker rule is a delayed, rare event (it matters in the last turns of a game), which makes it hard for a policy-gradient signal at this budget to find; we did not test that explanation. Imitating the saver first restores part of the rule (more than half of the wins go out with a joker), and the group-relative advantage keeps it stable across training seeds, but none of the eighteen variants of Table~\ref{tab:rl} moves the score against max-tiles beyond $0.50$--$0.55$, and against unseen opponents the network behaves like max-tiles (Table~\ref{tab:robustez}). Three of those variants are worth more than the rest, because they rule out the obvious diagnoses: a search given the \emph{true} hidden hands does not improve, so hidden information is not the binding constraint; wider layers and richer features change nothing, so neither is capacity or state; and rewarding the official score makes the network release jokers earlier, exactly as the heuristics say it should, yet leaves it $5.1$ points below max-tiles on the metric it optimises, so the reward is not it either. What remains is credit assignment for a rare event at the end of a hand, plus the strength of the baseline. The contrast with IRumAI \cite{mohan2026}, which reaches $53.9\%$ against a strong search baseline in Indian Rummy with the same clone-then-policy-gradient outline, suggests the difference lies in scale and observation design --- meld-aware encodings, a convolutional policy, deadwood shaping --- rather than in the recipe; we make no prediction about whether those ingredients would clear our baseline. A joker-aware state, an explanation of the rule in Jev's prompt, search with a different rollout policy, and reward that is dense only in the last turns of a hand are the natural next experiments.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:lim}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep0pt
\item \textbf{The objective and the format.} Every experiment other than Sections~\ref{sec:pontos} and~\ref{sec:sessao} scores a single hand as win, draw or loss, which is neither the metric printed in the rules nor the format in which the game is played. Those two sections cover the heuristics at two players only; the mechanism ablations, the league and the larger tables were all run on the $\pm1$ single-hand outcome. One set of networks was retrained with the official score as the reward (Section~\ref{sec:moves}): it releases jokers earlier, as predicted, but stays below max-tiles in points. No agent was trained on a session return, and the session experiment uses $K=10$ (with $K\in\{1,5,20\}$ in one cell), two players and one threshold for the adaptive rule. Sessions are also simulated as independent deals apart from the accumulated balance; we do not model opponents adapting across hands.
\item \textbf{Menu.} ``Best'' means best of seven hand-written strategies, plus five exploratory ones in Section~\ref{sec:liga}, three in Section~\ref{sec:pontos} and one in Section~\ref{sec:sessao}; stronger policies may exist. Our only search agent (PIMC) used few worlds, the joker-saver as rollout policy and $30$--$60$ seeds per comparison. The move network has a $16$-unit hidden layer; hyper-parameters were not tuned, and only one value each of the learning rate, group size ($4$), shaping weight ($0.5$) and wider league was tried.
\item \textbf{Joker identity on the table.} Officially a joker already melded must keep representing the same tile unless the player supplies that tile; our engine stores a joker as a value-free tile and validates only the final sets, so a rearrangement may silently change what a table joker represents. The ``jokers frozen on table'' ablation is stricter than the official rule and the default is more permissive, so the true rule lies between two conditions we measured, but was not itself measured. Since every main result concerns jokers, this is the fidelity gap we would close first.
\item \textbf{Truncated actions.} Rearrangement plans are a sample of $\le128$. Section~\ref{sec:cap} varies the cap for the seven heuristics only (ranking unchanged); Jev, PIMC and the move network keep the default cap, and even the largest cap is not a full search of the table.
\item \textbf{Two players.} All learning experiments and the PIMC agent, and the mechanism ablations other than the three-player check, are for two players; Section~\ref{sec:mesa} runs the heuristics and retrained learned agents at three and four, where the joker-saver's advantage shrinks (three players) or vanishes (four); the learned agents there have one training run per table size. The curriculum network of Section~\ref{sec:estresse} was trained on tables of two to ten players but evaluated only at $2$ to $6$ and at $10$ players; tables of seven, eight and nine were seen in training and never measured. All table sizes are simulated, with the same heuristic opponents; no human players.
\item \textbf{Draws and the rule used.} The tournament, mechanism, Q-meta and Jev results use the draw-at-pool-exhaustion rule; only the tournament, the joker-saver ablations and Q-meta were repeated under the official tie-break (Sections~\ref{sec:tie} and~\ref{sec:rlrule}); Jev-meta was not. Jev-moves and PIMC were run only under the official rule.
\item \textbf{Drawn jokers.} The effect of a drawn joker is an adjusted association: stratifying by the number of draws is needed because players who draw more are behind and also meet more jokers, but the number of draws is itself influenced by what the player holds, so the adjustment may remove part of a real effect. Only the initial deal is an exogenous assignment.
\item \textbf{Mechanism evidence is within-simulator.} The interventions are on policies, not on game rules, and part of the effect (the joint before/after-opening effect) is unexplained. The mechanism experiments were designed after the ranking and were not pre-registered.
\item \textbf{Statistics.} Normal-approximation intervals over seeds; tournament pairs are uncorrected; the Holm family covers only the mechanism table and the extended league.
\item \textbf{RL and Jev.} Q-meta has a coarse state and 1{,}200 episodes per replica; the Jev-meta sample is 600 games, one prompt, one model version, and $49\%$ of calls fell back. Jev-moves has $70$ seeds per opponent, one prompt and one model version, and is exploratory; the from-scratch move network is one training run (no seed replicas), so its distance from max-tiles is not separated from run-to-run variation; the cloned variants have three training seeds, but all RL variants were evaluated on the same simulator, with several looks at the data (a first estimate of $0.605$ against max-tiles did not replicate), and the $p$-values in Tables~\ref{tab:moves} and~\ref{tab:rl} are not corrected for multiplicity across rows. The three training seeds of each variant share deals, so pooled intervals are optimistic in the sense of \cite{agarwal2021}. Training the cloned networks with rearrangement on was run for one seed only.
\item \textbf{Untested techniques.} We did not run an asymmetric critic trained with hidden information, a model of the opponent's hand inside the search, or search with a learned value function (Section~\ref{sec:related}); the tile-counting tests covered two hand-written blocking rules and a $16$-unit network trained by REINFORCE, not other uses of the count; the negative results for learned agents are for the methods we ran, not for RL in general.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\textbf{RQ1.} In a paired tournament of seven strategies (3{,}360 games) the joker-saver ranks first ($0.726\pm0.027$), with max-tiles and max-points statistically tied behind it; never rearranging and cautious play are much worse. \textbf{RQ1b.} That ranking is an artefact of the win/draw/loss metric. Scored by official tile points on the same $18{,}000$ games, the joker-saver falls to sixth of ten, $5.6$ [$3.8$, $7.4$] points per game below max-tiles while still beating it in games won, because it ends its losses holding $0.80$ jokers at $30$ points each; Spearman between the two orderings is $0.64$. \textbf{RQ1c.} Over sessions of ten hands with accumulated points, the format the rules define, the inversion is complete: the joker-saver wins the most hands of any strategy ($5.4$ of $10$) and the fewest sessions ($0.288\pm0.030$), losing $72.5\%$ [$66.8$, $78.2$] of sessions to max-tiles, and the effect grows with session length ($0.558$ at $K{=}1$, $0.754$ at $K{=}5$ and $K{=}20$). The session ranking matches the per-hand points ranking (Spearman $+0.90$) and reverses the per-hand win ranking ($-1.00$). Releasing the joker when an opponent nears going out is first in both points and sessions won; switching to joker-saving when behind on the scoreboard loses ($0.444$), so the accumulated balance is information our rule turned into a deficit. \textbf{RQ2.} The joker-saver's advantage in hands won comes from holding a joker until it can complete the final move: soft penalties and table-recycling do not reproduce it, the effect arises mainly after the opening, and removing the finishing exception reverses it ($0.568\to0.189$); $71\%$ of its wins end with a joker. \textbf{RQ3.} A strategy-selecting Q-learner (6{,}000 training games), a Jev-based selector (600 games, US\$1.23), Jev choosing among all legal moves ($0.350$ and $0.414$ against max-tiles and the joker-saver), a small determinised search ($0.433$ against the joker-saver) and a policy network trained on the move list ($0.403$ against the joker-saver, $0.493$ against max-tiles) did not beat the best fixed heuristic. Starting the network from a clone of the joker-saver and using group-relative advantages brings it level with both ($0.550\pm0.040$ and $0.495\pm0.040$, exploratory, not significant after correction for the several variants tried); reward shaping, a wider training league, training with rearrangement and a residual network over the saver add nothing, Deep Monte Carlo stays below the saver ($0.47$) and PPO, level with it on development seeds ($0.50$), falls to $0.447$ on held-out seeds, a search that sees the true hidden hands also stays at $0.508$, and against twelve opponents (five unseen in training) it behaves like max-tiles. Rewarding the official score instead of the game outcome makes the network release jokers earlier ($0.22$ left in hand against $0.28$, $-33.2$ points per loss against $-38.7$) but leaves it $5.1$ points below max-tiles in points, so the negative result holds under both objectives. The joker-saver stays first under the official pool-exhaustion tie-break ($0.755\pm0.027$) and the finishing-tile intervention keeps its effect ($0.164$). In a pooled field of 8{,}000 hands, a starting joker is worth at most about two points on average and a drawn joker adds $+0.119$ only to the joker-saver. These conclusions hold for this simulator, action sample and strategy menu; they do not establish a universally optimal Rummikub strategy. The broader methodological point does not depend on Rummikub: the conventional metric (hands won) and the metric the rules define (points over a session) were anti-correlated on our menu, so a $0.573$ per-hand win rate meant losing three sessions in four.
