\title{What Does It Mean to Win? Metric Choice Reverses the Strategy Ranking in Rummikub}
\author{}
\markboth{IEEE Transactions on Games}{}
\IEEEtitleabstractindextext{%
\begin{abstract}
We study which simple strategy wins at two-player Rummikub, and find that the answer depends on how winning is measured. In a simulator of the official rules, a paired round-robin of seven interpretable strategies is won by the \emph{joker-saver}, which plays as many tiles as possible but spends a wildcard only to empty its hand. Four ablations ($13{,}000$ games) isolate the cause: forbidding the joker as the final tile reverses the advantage, and $71\%$ of the saver's wins end with a joker. Official Rummikub, however, is zero-sum in tile points, with a joker left in hand counting $30$. Scoring the same $18{,}000$ games both ways, the saver is first in games won and sixth of ten in points. Over sessions of ten hands with accumulated points, the format the rules define, the inversion is complete: the saver wins the most hands of any strategy and the fewest sessions, losing $72.5\%$ of sessions to max-tiles, and the session ranking is the exact reverse of the per-hand win ranking. A per-hand win rate of $0.573$ corresponded to losing three sessions in four. Twenty-four learned and search agents, including PPO, Deep Monte Carlo, a determinised search given the true hidden hands, training on the official reward, and per-decision potential shaping, fail to beat the best heuristic under either metric. Scaling the training budget $27\times$ to $576{,}000$ games produces the first learned agent to beat the champion in points ($+4.69$ $[+2.10,+7.28]$), and it does so by \emph{winning fewer hands} and losing more cheaply; it still trails the best heuristic ($-1.69$ paired in a $9{,}360$-game field; $-1.15$ $[-1.63,-0.67]$ head-to-head over $1{,}200$ hands), so the best Rummikub player here remains a three-line rule.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{R}{ummikub} is a tile game in which players empty their hands by forming runs and groups on a shared table. Its \emph{optimisation} subproblems are well studied: forming the best set of melds from a hand is polynomial-time solvable but becomes hard under generalisations \cite{vanrijn2016}, and integer linear programming solves full table-rearrangement problems \cite{denhertog2006}. Much less is known about which \emph{playing policies} win more often. Popular advice (``keep your jokers'') is widespread but, to our knowledge, untested.

This paper reports a result that began as a strategy study and became a measurement study. We first confirm the folk advice in its conditional form and explain it mechanistically: among seven interpretable strategies, the one that hoards wildcards until they can end the game wins the most games, and an intervention on that single exception reverses the effect. We then observe that the quantity we had been maximising --- games won --- is not the quantity the rules define. Official Rummikub is zero-sum in tile points, a joker left in hand counts $30$, and play is organised in sessions of several hands with accumulated scores. Re-scoring the same games, and then simulating sessions, reverses the ranking: the policy that wins the most hands wins the fewest sessions.

We ask four research questions.
\begin{description}\itemsep0pt
\item[RQ1] Among a menu of interpretable strategies, which wins most often?
\item[RQ2] What \emph{mechanism} explains the winner's advantage?
\item[RQ3] Does the answer survive the scoring metric printed in the rules, and the multi-hand format in which the game is played?
\item[RQ4] Can reinforcement learning (RL), a language-model decision-maker or a search agent beat the best fixed strategy, under either metric?
\end{description}

\paragraph*{Status of the evidence}
RQ1 is exploratory. The RQ2 experiments were designed after seeing the RQ1 result, with four competing explanations and a prediction for each, and are corrected as one multiple-comparison family; they were not pre-registered. The two RQ3 experiments and the official-reward run of RQ4 have development and test seeds with criteria fixed in writing before any data were collected, and the test seeds were spent once. All results are conditional on this simulator and this strategy menu. Supplementary material (anonymised repository) contains per-game records and every analysis script.

\section{Related Work}
\paragraph*{Rummikub}
The literature we found treats Rummikub as an optimisation problem: a polynomial-time algorithm for the one-hand puzzle \cite{vanrijn2016}, an integer-programming formulation for rearranging the table \cite{denhertog2006}, a thesis counting winning hands \cite{feijen2023}, and one that reduces the solver's memory and classifies puzzle difficulty \cite{wekken2025}. The last explicitly notes that the multi-player game (initial meld, table manipulation, strategy) has received little study and leaves strategy for future work. We found no study evaluating learning or search agents on the full multi-player game against heuristic baselines; this is the result of a limited search, not a proof of absence. The closest learning study we know is on a sibling game: temporal-difference learning with self-play lost to co-evolution of the value function in gin rummy \cite{kotnik2003}.

\paragraph*{Learning in large-action card games}
DouZero reaches strong DouDizhu play by Monte Carlo returns, a network that scores encoded (state, action) pairs, and many parallel actors, which suits action sets that change from turn to turn \cite{zha2021}; our move network likewise scores concrete moves rather than using a fixed action head. Suphx adds global reward prediction and oracle guiding to deep RL for mahjong \cite{li2020}. Policy-gradient self-play against a pool of past checkpoints is common practice in tabletop-game RL \cite{balla2024}, which our league runs follow. A large-scale study finds generic PPO \cite{schulman2017} competitive with game-theoretic deep-RL methods in imperfect-information games \cite{rudolph2025}. Group-relative advantages, which replace a learned critic by standardising returns within a group of rollouts sharing a prompt, were introduced for language-model training \cite{shao2024}; we use the same estimator with a group of games sharing a deal. Our shaping term is a potential difference and therefore policy-invariant in the sense of \cite{ng1999}. The closest positive result to our own setup is IRumAI, which warm-starts PPO by behaviour cloning in Indian Rummy and reaches a $53.9\%$ win rate against the strongest search baseline unseen in training \cite{mohan2026}; our recipe is the same in outline (clone, then policy gradient) and does not reproduce that outcome, which we discuss in Section~\ref{sec:disc}.

\paragraph*{Search under hidden information}
Perfect-information Monte Carlo (PIMC) sampling, which our search agent follows, is practical but suffers strategy fusion, since the search may choose different continuations in worlds the player cannot distinguish \cite{frank1998}; its empirical success has been characterised \cite{long2010}, and information-set MCTS searches the player's information set instead of sampled worlds \cite{cowling2012}.

\paragraph*{Evaluation}
Our methodological conclusion is adjacent to work arguing that deep-RL comparisons rely too heavily on point estimates from few runs \cite{agarwal2021}. That critique concerns \emph{statistical} reliability at a fixed metric. We report a case where the metric itself, rather than its variance, reverses the conclusion.

\section{Simulator and Strategies}
\paragraph*{Game}
Two players in the main experiments; $106$ tiles ($4$ colours $\times$ values $1$--$13$ $\times$ $2$ copies, plus $2$ jokers); $14$ tiles per hand. A turn either draws one tile or lays tiles down. The first lay-down (the \emph{opening meld}) must be worth at least $30$ points using only the player's own tiles and may not touch the table. Afterwards a player may lay several melds, extend melds, and rearrange the whole table, provided every meld is valid at the end of the turn and at least one tile from the hand was played. The two jokers are shuffled with the other tiles. A hand ends when a rack is empty; if the pool is empty and all players pass in succession, it ends as well. Seat~0 moves first. The engine supports two to ten players and was validated against an independent third-party solver (Section~\ref{sec:rob}).

\paragraph*{Scoring}
Three rules appear in this paper. \textbf{Game count}: win $=1$, draw $=0.5$, loss $=0$, the convention we started with. \textbf{Official points} \cite{pagat2024}: the player who goes out scores the sum of all tiles left on opponents' racks, each loser scores minus its own rack, a joker counting $30$; if the pool empties with nobody out, the lowest rack wins the differences. Both are zero-sum. \textbf{Session}: $K$ hands, official points accumulated, highest total wins.

\paragraph*{Action space}
At each decision the simulator lists \emph{draw}, \emph{lay down new melds}, and \emph{rearrange} actions. Because exhaustive table search is expensive, rearrangements are a sample of at most $128$ plans; this is the main fidelity limitation (Sections~\ref{sec:rob} and~\ref{sec:lim}).

\paragraph*{Strategies}
Each chooses one legal action. The original seven are in Table~\ref{tab:strat}; nine more are introduced where they are first used.

\section{Protocol and Statistics}
\paragraph*{Pairing}
For every seed, all seatings are played with the same deal; the unit of analysis is the per-seed mean score of the strategy of interest. Pairing cancels the first-mover and deal effects: a strategy played against itself scores exactly $0.5$ by construction, which gives a real control row rather than a nominal one. Seat~0 wins $50.8\%\pm1.5\%$ of tournament games. In the session experiments the seed of hand $k$ is derived from (session seed, $k$), so different strategies meet the same hands in the same position of a session, and the starting seat rotates each hand as the turn to start passes in the rules.

\paragraph*{Intervals and tests}
We report mean $\pm 1.96$ standard errors across seeds. In the mechanism family ($13$ groups, two of them mirror controls with $p=1$ by construction) $p$-values are Holm-corrected \cite{holm1979}; the confirmatory experiments of Sections~\ref{sec:metric} and~\ref{sec:session} are Holm-corrected within their own families. Tournament intervals are descriptive. Where observations are clustered (two hands of a game, games of a seed) we use percentile bootstrap over seeds.

\paragraph*{Scale}
$123{,}000$ hands of two-player play across all experiments, of which $18{,}000$ are the dual-metric tournament and $36{,}000$ the session test; $36{,}000$ training games for the move network plus nineteen further training runs; three- to ten-player tables; and $2{,}100$ games against an external solver. Seed ranges are disjoint by experiment and listed in the supplement.

\subsection{Mechanism}\label{sec:mech}
The joker-saver differs from max-tiles in exactly one respect, so the question is which part of that behaviour matters. We stated four candidate explanations and a prediction for each, then tested all four against max-tiles as opponent ($500$ seeds $\times$ $2$ seats per row, Table~\ref{tab:mec}, Fig.~\ref{fig:mec}).

\textbf{E1, a mild preference for spending fewer jokers}, is not supported. Soft penalties of $0.5$--$4$ tiles per joker score $0.500$--$0.508$, none distinguishable from $0.5$ after correction, and a joker \emph{bonus} is identical to the control. The reason is visible in the telemetry: with soft penalties the player still spends $0.77$ jokers per game, as often as the control ($0.78$), because a preference only breaks ties between moves of equal size. The saver's behaviour is qualitatively different: it \emph{refuses} joker moves and draws instead, ending an average game with $0.31$ jokers in hand.

\textbf{E2, recycling jokers on the table}, is not supported. When jokers can never be removed from table melds, the saver scores $0.581\pm0.027$, no lower than under standard rules ($0.568\pm0.027$).

\textbf{E3, timing}, partly holds. Saving only before the opening gives nothing ($0.495\pm0.021$); saving only after it gives $0.537\pm0.022$ ($p_{\text{Holm}}=0.01$). The two timings do not add up to the full rule, so part of the effect requires both phases; we have not isolated it.

\textbf{E4, the joker as a finishing tile}, is supported. Forbidding the saver to use a joker even to empty its hand collapses it to $0.189\pm0.021$: it ends games holding $0.86$ jokers and never goes out with one. Conversely, $71\%$ of the full saver's wins end with a joker in the final move, against $4\%$ for max-tiles. Because the two variants differ \emph{only} in that exception, this is an intervention on the policy rather than a correlation.

So the folk advice is half right: unconditional saving is far worse than not saving, and the condition ``unless it wins the game'' carries the whole effect. A held wildcard keeps an awkward hand completable; this is our reading of E3--E4, and we did not test the narrower claim about awkward last tiles.

\section{RQ3: The Metric and the Format}
\subsection{Official points}\label{sec:metric}
Everything above counts games. Official Rummikub counts tile points, and the joker-saver is precisely the policy for which the two may disagree: it ends its losses holding $0.80$ jokers, a $24$-point liability per lost game. We therefore replayed the tournament computing both metrics \emph{from the same games}, so they are perfectly paired, and added three strategies that decide when to release a joker, defined before running: \emph{saver-then-dump} (the saver, but once the pool is empty it plays the largest move even with a joker), \emph{saver-dump-near} (the saver, but it stops sparing jokers once an opponent holds three tiles or fewer) and \emph{joker-spender} (prefers moves that spend jokers; a control for the opposite extreme). Criteria were fixed on development seeds ($120$ seeds); the test seeds ($200$ seeds $\times$ $2$ seats, $18{,}000$ games) were spent once with Holm over three hypotheses.

The ranking changes (Table~\ref{tab:pontos}, Fig.~\ref{fig:pontos}). The joker-saver is first by win score ($0.680\pm0.017$) and sixth of ten by points ($+13.3\pm1.5$), below max-tiles and max-points ($+19.5$, $+19.6$), which it beats in games won. Spearman's correlation between the two orderings is $0.64$, against $0.96$ between alternative end-of-game rules (Section~\ref{sec:rob}): the metric reorders the top far more than the rule does. Against a common field of five strategies, paired by seed, max-tiles is $+5.59$ $[+3.78,+7.39]$ points above the saver while being $-0.013\pm0.018$ below it in win score (criterion $\ge+3.0$ with a positive interval; met, $p_{\text{Holm}}<0.001$). Head-to-head the saver wins $0.573\pm0.044$ of games and still loses $5.4\pm3.1$ points per game.

The cause mirrors E4. Decomposing the saver's points against the common field: it wins $77.8\%$ of those games, and when it wins it collects $+48.0$ points, the same as max-tiles ($+47.9$) --- holding a joker buys the win, not a bigger win. When it loses it pays $-43.3$ against $-14.6$ for max-tiles, and $24$ of those $29$ extra points are the retained joker.

Intervening on the policy confirms the channel. \emph{Saver-then-dump} ends its losses with $0.64$ jokers instead of $0.80$, gaining $+2.83$ $[+1.99,+3.67]$ points \emph{and} $+0.019$ $[+0.012,+0.025]$ win score: releasing the joker once the pool is empty is free. The points gain fell short of the $+3.0$ fixed in advance, so we report it as a near miss. \emph{Saver-dump-near} releases much earlier, ends with $0.09$ jokers, pays $-17.7$ per loss, and is first of all ten in points ($+19.8\pm1.1$) while sixth in win score. \emph{Joker-spender} is inert: under max-tiles play a joker is in hand in $2.6\%$ of decisions and a choice between a joker move and a non-joker move of equal size arises in $0.1\%$, so it played identically to max-tiles in all $1{,}440$ comparable games. That is a property of the action distribution, and it explains why the joker-bonus rows of Table~\ref{tab:mec} equal the control.

\subsection{Sessions of several hands}\label{sec:session}
Rummikub is normally played as a session: several deals, points accumulated, highest total wins. Summing hands narrows the distribution around the mean, and the saver's mean is negative, so the session should amplify the effect. A session also creates information no single-hand strategy has, the accumulated balance, so we added \emph{session-adaptive}: when behind by more than one joker ($-30$ points, fixed in advance) it plays the joker-saver, maximising its chance of winning the hand; otherwise it plays saver-dump-near. Six strategies, $15$ pairs, $K=10$, both seat arrangements; development seeds ($80$ sessions per pair) then test seeds once ($120$ sessions per pair, $3{,}600$ sessions, $36{,}000$ hands), Holm over two primary criteria.

The inversion is complete (Table~\ref{tab:sessao}, Fig.~\ref{fig:sessao}). The joker-saver wins $5.39$ of the $10$ hands in a session, more than any other strategy, and $0.288\pm0.030$ of the sessions, fewer than any other strategy. Head-to-head against max-tiles it takes $5.53$ hands of $10$ and loses $72.5\%$ $[66.8,78.2]$ of the sessions (criterion $\ge0.55$ with the interval above $0.5$; met, $p_{\text{Holm}}<0.001$) at $-75.6\pm14.9$ points per session. The session ranking matches the per-hand \emph{points} ranking (Spearman $+0.90$) and is the exact reverse of the per-hand \emph{win} ranking (Spearman $-1.00$) on the five strategies shared by both experiments. The effect grows with session length, as the arithmetic predicts: max-tiles beats the saver in $0.558\pm0.089$ of one-hand sessions, $0.754\pm0.077$ at $K=5$ and $0.754\pm0.077$ at $K=20$, with the points margin rising from $+14$ to $+142$.

\emph{Session-adaptive} failed, in the informative direction. It loses to saver-dump-near, the fixed strategy it switches away from ($0.444$ $[0.414,0.473]$; criterion not met, and the deficit itself is significant, $p_{\text{Holm}}=0.0002$). The rule is not inert: it is behind the threshold in $26\%$ of hands. Switching to joker-saving when behind buys hands ($4.96$ per session, the most of the four strong strategies) and costs points. Chasing a deficit by saving jokers digs the hole deeper; the accumulated balance is real information that this rule turns into a loss, and we report it rather than tuning a threshold fixed in advance at one value.

\section{RQ4: Learned and Searching Agents}
We ran twenty-four agents that choose either a strategy from the menu or a concrete move (Table~\ref{tab:rl}). None beats the best heuristic. Rather than narrate each, we give the five results that carry information beyond ``no gain'': what the architecture can find, an estimate that did not replicate, the two excuses that fail, what $27\times$ more compute buys, and what per-decision credit does not.

\paragraph*{The architecture can represent the mechanism; the search does not find it}
The move network scores each legal move from $15$ features (move size, jokers used, hand and pool sizes, opening status, and so on) through one hidden layer of $16$ tanh units, trained by REINFORCE against a league of heuristics, itself, and past checkpoints. Trained from scratch over $36{,}000$ games it converges to max-tiles-like play: $0.493\pm0.029$ against max-tiles, $0.403\pm0.030$ against the saver, going out with a joker in $2.9\%$ of games against the saver's $41\%$. Supervised cloning of the saver on $10{,}587$ of its decisions reaches $82\%$ agreement on held-out decisions and reproduces the mechanism ($70\%$ of its wins end with a joker), which shows the features suffice to express the rule. What the from-scratch run lacked was the search for it. The candidate explanation is credit assignment: the reward arrives once per hand, after roughly sixty decisions, so the decision that matters is averaged with the ones that do not. We tested it and it did not hold (Section~\ref{sec:passo}). We also measured the premise: a real choice between playing and keeping a joker arises in $17.3\%$ of decisions ($4{,}024$ sampled) and keeping it always costs tiles, so it is a recurring trade-off, not a rare end-of-hand event.

\paragraph*{One positive estimate did not replicate}
Cloning the saver and then refining it produced, on the first training seed, $0.605$ against max-tiles --- the only number in this project that appeared to beat both heuristics. Replicating with two further training seeds and evaluating on fresh seeds gave $0.509\pm0.045$: a favourable draw of seeds and run, selected by looking. Adding group-relative advantages \cite{shao2024} with four games per deal reaches $0.550\pm0.040$ against max-tiles ($p=0.014$ uncorrected), the only interval in the paper excluding $0.5$ against max-tiles, and we read it cautiously: it is one of four comparisons, and its difference from the moving-average baseline on the same seeds is not itself significant. Grouping does stabilise the mechanism, with $59$--$66\%$ of wins ending with a joker across all three trainings against $14$--$43\%$ without it. PPO showed the same pattern at a larger scale, scoring $0.500$ against the saver on development seeds and $0.447$ on held-out seeds used once.

\paragraph*{Neither hidden information nor the reward is the binding constraint}
Two experiments test the two most plausible excuses. Giving the determinised search the \emph{true} world --- the opponent's hand and the draw order --- instead of sampled ones leaves it at $0.508\pm0.089$ against the saver, so perfect information about the hidden state does not lift this one-step search above the heuristic. And since Section~\ref{sec:metric} shows game count is the secondary metric, we retrained with reward $=\text{points}/60$ from the same clones. The networks learn exactly what the heuristics say is worth points: they end a hand holding $0.22$ jokers against the control's $0.28$, go out with a joker in $58\%$ of wins against $69\%$, and pay $-33.2$ points per loss against $-38.7$. But they gain only $+1.7$ $[-0.7,+4.1]$ points over the control, short of the $+3$ fixed in advance, and remain $5.1$ points \emph{below} max-tiles on the metric they optimise. The one behaviour the official reward teaches is a behaviour we can also write in one line of heuristic, where it is worth $4.5$ points. What limits these agents is neither the information nor which number we reward.

\paragraph*{Scaling the budget $27\times$ changes the mechanism, not the ranking}\label{sec:orcamento}
Every row of Table~\ref{tab:rl} trains on $7{,}200$ games, a limitation we had not measured. We repeated the best recipe changing \emph{only} the budget: $192{,}000$ games per seed, three seeds, $576{,}000$ games, $9.9$~h on six cores, criteria fixed in advance, held-out seeds reserved. Against the joker-saver the scaled agent scores $+4.69$ $[+2.10,+7.28]$ official points, the first learned agent here whose interval excludes zero against the champion on the metric the rules define. Its isolated gain over the same recipe at the old budget was $+2.83$ $[-0.84,+6.51]$ on $150$ hands, short of the $+5$ we had required; re-measured on $1{,}200$ fresh hands (seeds $300{,}000$+, enough for $\pm1$ point) it is $+3.57$ $[+2.24,+4.90]$, real and still below the bar.

The mechanism inverts the usual story and is the reason we report this at all: the scaled agent \emph{wins fewer hands} than its own control ($0.390$ against $0.487$) yet scores more points, because it loses far more cheaply ($-18.1$ against $-42.5$ per loss). In defeat it ends with $2.78$ tiles and $0.011$ jokers in hand, against $4.31$ and $0.604$ for the control: it learned to release the joker and empty its hand \emph{before} losing rather than chase the win. That is precisely the policy Section~\ref{sec:metric} says the official metric rewards, found by search rather than written by us, and no heuristic on our menu implements it. The budget also stopped binding early --- training return saturated near iteration $450$ of $4{,}000$, and the last $167{,}000$ games bought nothing --- so $27\times$ the games bought about $3.6$ points and then nothing, which removes budget from the list of explanations for the null results, alongside capacity, information and reward.

\emph{Is it then the best player? No: it trails the best by about a point.} A duel against two opponents cannot settle that, so we entered the three networks into the common-field tournament of Section~\ref{sec:metric}: thirteen competitors, every pair, the same hands, both seats, $9{,}360$ games, seeds $270{,}000$+. The network ranks \emph{first} in official points, $+16.96\pm1.38$ against max-tiles' $+15.60\pm1.44$ --- and that ranking is an artefact. The paired comparison on identical hands against the same nine common opponents gives $-1.69$ $[-3.70,+0.31]$: the interval includes zero and the sign is negative. Head-to-head on $150$ hands this looked like a tie ($-0.60$ $[-2.75,+1.55]$ points, $0.492$ of hands); on $1{,}200$ fresh hands it is a small real loss, $-1.15$ $[-1.63,-0.67]$ points and $0.472\pm0.012$ of hands, and in win rate it is sixth of eleven. What survives is narrower: it beats the joker-saver in points head-to-head, $+5.59$ $[+1.42,+9.75]$, the only learned agent here to do so. The rank-order lead comes from each agent facing a slightly different field, which is why we report the paired difference rather than the ranking.

\paragraph*{Per-decision credit does not lift the agent either}\label{sec:passo}
Every result above trains on one scalar per hand, so each of the roughly sixty decisions of a hand receives the same credit and the turn that keeps a joker is indistinguishable from a turn with one legal move. This is the explanation we had been offering for the nulls, and it is testable. We gave each decision its own reward by potential shaping \cite{ng1999}: $r_t=\Phi(s_{t+1})-\Phi(s_t)$ with $\Phi=-(\text{tile points in hand}+30\cdot\text{jokers in hand})/60$, so releasing a joker is worth $+0.5$ at the step it happens. The sum telescopes to a policy-independent constant, so the optimal policy is unchanged (verified on sampled games to $2.2\times10^{-16}$). Treatment and control share the clone and differ only in this flag: three seeds each, $48{,}000$ games per seed, criterion fixed at $\ge+3$ points with the interval excluding zero.

It failed, in the unfavourable direction: $-2.12$ $[-5.43,+1.20]$ points against its own control, and $-1.94\pm1.47$ against max-tiles where the control scores $-0.12\pm1.36$. The intervention was applied (the flag is in each run manifest; the L2 distance between treatment and control weights is $1.59$), so this is a negative about the intervention, not a misconfiguration. The held-out seeds were not spent.

Three readings stay open. $\Phi$ rewards emptying the hand, which is what max-tiles already does, so it plausibly pushed the network \emph{towards} greedy play --- consistent with the treatment sitting further below max-tiles than the control. The budget may be short for a dense signal, though Section~\ref{sec:orcamento} measured saturation at a quarter of it. Or credit assignment was not the binding constraint. What is defensible is that the theoretically correct fix, implemented and verified, did not help.

\paragraph*{A language model as decision-maker}
We also used a commercial System One model as a comparator, in two roles. As a strategy selector over $600$ games it chose max-tiles in $84\%$ of answered decisions and ``draw'' in $16\%$, never selecting the joker-saver, and scored $0.500$ against max-tiles: in this protocol it \emph{is} max-tiles. Given instead every legal move of the turn, with each option's concrete consequence computed in code (median $30$ options), it scored $0.350\pm0.076$ against max-tiles, playing a joker in $3$--$4\%$ of its moves. The prompt stated the rules but no strategy advice, never mentioned the finishing-joker mechanism, and was not tuned on results. Total spend was US\$$3.1$.

\section{Robustness}\label{sec:rob}
Five checks support the game-count results of Sections~\ref{sec:mech} and~\ref{sec:metric}; all are reported in full in the supplement.

\textbf{End-of-game rule.} Our main experiments score a stalled pool as a draw ($16.3\%$ of games). Replaying the same $3{,}360$ games under the official tie-break and under fewest-tiles leaves the top of the ranking and the mechanism unchanged (Spearman $0.96$ for both; the saver's lead grows to $0.755$ and the finishing-tile ablation stays at $0.164$). The only reordering is at the bottom, where no-rearrange falls from $0.271$ to $0.010$: $54\%$ of its games ended in pool exhaustion, so the draw rule had been flattering the weakest strategy.

\textbf{Rearrangement cap.} The $128$-plan cap is reached in $9.9\%$ of decisions and $73\%$ of games, so it is not harmless. Replaying with caps of $512$, $2{,}048$ and $4{,}096$ plans leaves the ranking identical in all four columns and \emph{increases} the saver's head-to-head edge ($0.525\to0.625$), so truncation understates rather than creates the effect. An offline oracle re-enumerating $383$ capped states found a play with more tiles in $4.2\%$ of them, always by exactly one tile.

\textbf{A wider menu.} A $12$-strategy league ($66$ pairs, $10{,}560$ games) keeps the saver first by mean score and Bradley--Terry strength, though the three variants that keep a joker to the end are not separable from each other. No pair forms a three-way cycle, so strength is transitive on this menu. The finishing-joker pattern reappears: $59$--$62\%$ of the wins of the saver and its closest variant end with a joker, against $0$--$18\%$ for the others.

\textbf{More players.} At three players the saver keeps a smaller edge ($0.374$ against chance $0.333$); at four it is exactly at chance ($0.249$), so the advantage vanishes as hands get shorter. The finishing-joker ablation still collapses at three players ($0.159$), so the mechanism holds where the advantage exists. Networks retrained with a curriculum over tables of two to ten players gain $+0.04$ to $+0.06$ at four, six and ten players on held-out seeds, and a network trained \emph{only} on six-to-ten-player tables is indistinguishable from the curriculum one, so what matters is having seen large tables rather than the ones measured.

\textbf{An independent opponent.} All matches above are between policies we wrote, so we also played against third-party code: an open-source Rummikub solver \cite{rummikubsolver} built on the integer-programming formulation of \cite{denhertog2006}, whose default rule set matches ours exactly, confirming that our engine is standard Rummikub. Wrapped in the obvious greedy player (ask for the best placement; play it if it melds anything, otherwise draw) it wins $0.040$ $[0.015,0.064]$ of two-player games against the saver and essentially none in three- and four-player fields. Optimal per-turn placement is not a policy: the solver melds $13$ tiles per game and draws about $40$ times, where the saver melds $21$. Our trained networks beat it in essentially every game. This uses the solver outside its role as a one-turn advisor, but shows our strategies are strong against code we did not write.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:disc}
\paragraph*{On Rummikub}
The simulation supports a narrow, mechanistic answer to what wins a single hand by game count: play the biggest legal move, but keep jokers until one lets you go out. Three competing explanations fail and the finishing-tile intervention both removes and reverses the effect. But the practical advice inverts with the format. Under the scoring printed in the rules, keeping a joker costs about six points a hand against simply playing the most tiles, and over a session the same policy wins the most hands and the fewest sessions. The mechanism is one object seen from two sides: a held wildcard is a bet that pays a win and charges $30$ points when it fails, and which side dominates is decided by the scoring rule and the format, not by the game. Releasing the joker before the end improves points and sessions at once, so the frontier is not a strict trade-off everywhere.

\paragraph*{On measurement}
The methodological point does not depend on Rummikub. Game count is the cheap, low-variance, conventional metric; points over a session is the one the rules define. On our menu they were \emph{anti-correlated}: a per-hand win rate of $0.573$, the kind of margin this literature routinely reports as an improvement, corresponded to losing three sessions in four, and the session ranking was the exact reverse of the per-hand win ranking. No number of additional seeds on the cheap metric would have revealed this, which distinguishes the problem from the statistical-reliability concerns of \cite{agarwal2021}: more runs sharpen an estimate of the wrong quantity. The practical recommendation is modest but concrete: when a game defines a score, measure it, and when it is played in sessions, simulate sessions, even when a per-episode proxy is available and cheaper. Where the proxy and the defined objective disagree, report both.

\paragraph*{On the learning results}
Twenty-four agents fail to beat the best heuristic under both metrics, and the eliminations are the informative part: the limiting factor is not the capacity to express the saver's rule (a clone reproduces it from the same features; this does not show that the network can express anything better than the saver), not hidden information (a search given the true world does not improve), not the reward (training on official points changes behaviour correctly but far too little), and not the training budget ($27\times$ more games saturates after $13\%$ of them). What remains, after Section~\ref{sec:passo} removes credit assignment as well, is the strength of the heuristic baseline and the design of the observation itself: every feature we give the network is an aggregate scalar, which discards the two relations that define the game, since adjacent numbers form runs and distinct colours of one number form groups. We state the first as the leading explanation, not a proven one: with capacity, hidden information, reward, budget and per-decision credit each eliminated by a separate experiment \emph{within one family of agents} (a $273$-parameter network over $15$ aggregate features, trained by Monte Carlo policy gradient), what stands is that max-tiles is close to optimal for two-player Rummikub on this menu --- a claim about the game, not about reinforcement learning. The contrast with IRumAI \cite{mohan2026}, which reaches $53.9\%$ against a strong search baseline in Indian Rummy with the same clone-then-policy-gradient outline, points to scale and observation design rather than the recipe; their own ablation makes a meld-aware convolutional encoder their largest single contributor, worth $8$--$11$ percentage points over a width-matched MLP. We make no prediction about whether it would clear our baseline, but it is where we would look next, and the budget result rules out simply spending more compute on the representation we have. A joker-aware state, an explanation of the mechanism in the language model's prompt, search with a different rollout policy, and reward that is dense only in the last turns of a hand are the natural next experiments.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:lim}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep0pt
\item \textbf{Menu.} ``Best'' means best of sixteen hand-written strategies; stronger policies may exist. The move network has a $16$-unit hidden layer and hyper-parameters were not tuned; only one value each of the learning rate, group size, shaping weight and adaptive threshold was tried. The budget experiment scaled games, not capacity or hyper-parameters, so it bounds the effect of games alone. The shaping experiment tried one potential function, $-$(hand liability), at one budget; a potential built on formable melds rather than on hand liability is a different experiment and we did not run it. We also did not test whether a meld-aware observation would change the picture.
\item \textbf{Joker identity on the table.} Officially a melded joker must keep representing the same tile unless the player supplies that tile. Our engine stores a joker as a value-free tile and validates only the final sets, so a rearrangement can silently change what a table joker represents. Our frozen-joker ablation is stricter than the official rule and the default is more permissive, so the true rule lies between two conditions we measured but was not itself measured. Since every main result concerns jokers, this is the fidelity gap we would close first.
\item \textbf{Scope of the metric results.} The dual-metric tournament and the sessions are two players only, and the sessions use $K=10$ (with $K\in\{1,5,20\}$ in one cell) and model hands as independent apart from the accumulated balance; we do not model opponents adapting across hands. The mechanism ablations, the league and the larger tables were run on game count.
\item \textbf{Statistics.} Normal-approximation intervals over seeds; the Holm families cover the mechanism table, the league, and each confirmatory experiment separately, not the paper as a whole. Table~\ref{tab:rl} is uncorrected across rows, and the three training seeds of each learned variant share deals, so their pooled intervals are optimistic in the sense of \cite{agarwal2021}.
\item \textbf{Simulation only.} No human players, and the language-model results are one prompt and one pinned model version.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
Among seven interpretable Rummikub strategies, hoarding wildcards until one can end the hand wins the most hands ($0.726\pm0.027$), and an intervention on that single exception reverses the advantage ($0.568\to0.189$), with $71\%$ of its wins ending with a joker. Scored by the tile points the rules define, the same policy falls to sixth of ten, $5.6$ points per hand below simply playing the most tiles, because it ends its losses holding $0.80$ jokers at $30$ points each. Played as sessions of ten hands with accumulated points, it wins the most hands of any strategy and the fewest sessions, losing $72.5\%$ of sessions to max-tiles, and the session ranking is the exact reverse of the per-hand win ranking. Twenty-four learned and searching agents, including PPO, Deep Monte Carlo, a search given the true hidden hands, training on the official reward, a $27\times$ larger budget, and per-decision potential shaping, fail to beat the best heuristic under either metric; the largest-budget agent is the first to beat the champion in points, and it gets there by winning fewer hands and losing more cheaply, yet it still trails the strongest heuristic by about one point per hand. The strategy result is specific to this simulator and menu. The measurement result is not: the conventional metric and the one the rules define were anti-correlated here, and only simulating the format the game is played in revealed it.

\section*{Reproducibility}
Code, per-game records for every table, and all analysis scripts are in the anonymised supplementary repository, \repoanon. Every experiment is a subcommand of one entry point, and each confirmatory experiment fixed its success criterion and its seed ranges before running; seed ranges are disjoint and documented per experiment. The language-model API key is read from the environment and is not stored.
